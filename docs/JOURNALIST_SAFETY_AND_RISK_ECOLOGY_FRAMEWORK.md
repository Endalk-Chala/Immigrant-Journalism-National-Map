# Journalist Safety and Risk Ecology: Theoretical Framework

## Purpose

This memo positions *Epistemic Precarity: Risk Ecologies of Immigrant Journalism in the Emerging U.S. Local News Ecosystem* within the broader scholarship on journalist safety, occupational risk, resilience, organizational response, and sociotechnical vulnerability.

The project does not treat journalist safety as synonymous with physical protection, nor does it treat risk as a list of discrete incidents. Instead, it builds on a growing body of scholarship that understands safety as multidimensional, infrastructural, organizational, and relational, while extending that literature to the institutional ecology of immigrant-serving journalism in the United States.

---

## 1. From physical protection to multidimensional journalist safety

Early and policy-oriented approaches to journalist safety often centered the most visible forms of harm: killing, imprisonment, assault, abduction, and conflict-zone exposure. These remain foundational concerns, but contemporary scholarship increasingly treats occupational safety as multidimensional.

Slavtcheva-Petkova, Ramaprasad, Springer, Hughes, Hanitzsch, Hoxha, Hamada, and Steindl conceptualize journalist safety as comprising four interrelated dimensions: **physical, psychological, digital, and financial safety**. Their framework also distinguishes risk factors operating at societal or macro, organizational or meso, and individual or micro levels. Safety is therefore not simply the absence of bodily harm; it concerns journalists' ability to perform professional work without threats to bodily, psychological, technological, or economic integrity.

This multidimensional approach is essential to the present project because immigrant-serving journalists may face exposure that cannot be captured through assault or detention records alone. Legal uncertainty, digital harassment, lack of organizational support, unstable funding, platform dependency, source vulnerability, and psychological burden can all affect the conditions under which journalism is produced.

---

## 2. Newsafety: infrastructures, practices, and consequences

Westlund, Krøvel, and Orgeret's concept of **Newsafety** provides a second important foundation. Their sociotechnical framework organizes safety around three dimensions: **infrastructures, practices, and consequences**. The framework recognizes that contemporary journalism is inseparable from technological systems and platforms and that the same infrastructures that enable reporting, visibility, and distribution can also generate new vulnerabilities.

Newsafety is particularly useful for this project because it moves safety analysis beyond an individual journalist's conduct. It directs attention to the infrastructures through which journalists work, the practices they develop to manage risk, and the consequences that follow when safety is compromised.

For immigrant-serving outlets, those infrastructures include not only devices and cybersecurity systems but also social-media platforms, search and referral systems, WhatsApp and other messaging channels, donor and grant infrastructures, fiscal sponsors, professional networks, legal-support arrangements, and multilingual community relationships.

---

## 3. Safety as a research field: risk, resistance, and resilience

The 2023 *Journalism Studies* special issue **“Safety of Journalists: Risks, Resistance, Resilience,”** guest edited by Kristin Skare Orgeret and Roy Krøvel, further consolidated safety of journalists as a distinct research subfield. Its contributions examine how threats emerge, how journalists and organizations adapt, and how resilience and resistance become part of journalistic practice.

Frey's work on preparing for risk and building resilience foregrounds anticipation and preparedness, while Mesquita and de-Lima-Santos show that safety measures themselves can expand the boundaries of journalism. Their study of small- and medium-sized investigative organizations in Latin America finds that journalists absorb new responsibilities involving digital, physical, legal, and psychological protection, sometimes requiring collaboration with actors outside conventional journalism.

This literature matters for the present project because it makes organizational response analytically visible. Safety is not only a condition experienced by journalists; it becomes something news organizations must produce through policies, routines, training, partnerships, legal resources, technology, and decisions about whether particular reporting assignments can be pursued safely.

---

## 4. Organizational responsibility and the unequal distribution of safety work

Research on online harassment and occupational hazard strengthens the organizational dimension of the project.

Holton, Bélair-Gagnon, Bossio, and Molyneux show that harassment can produce fatigue, anxiety, withdrawal from social media, and professional disconnection. Crucially, journalists in their U.S. study frequently described insufficient organizational responses, leaving preventive and coping labor to individual journalists.

Bélair-Gagnon, Searles, Vraga, Holton, and Tandoc subsequently conceptualize attacks on journalism as an **occupational hazard**, explicitly shifting attention from individualized coping toward institutional intervention. This move is central to the present study: two journalists may encounter similar threats but experience very different consequences depending on the institutional resources surrounding them.

The analytical question therefore becomes not simply *Who was threatened?* but also:

- What organizational resources existed before the event?
- Who carried responsibility for anticipating and managing exposure?
- Was legal, safety, psychological, digital, or financial support available?
- Could the newsroom redistribute work or absorb a disruption?
- Did a protective response require sacrificing coverage, audience access, revenue, or professional autonomy?

## 5. Mental health, well-being, and the safety burden

Journalist-safety scholarship increasingly recognizes psychological safety and well-being as central rather than peripheral. Research associated with Valérie Bélair-Gagnon and colleagues at the University of Minnesota's Hubbard School has examined harassment, occupational risk, coping, well-being, digital hostility, and the institutional responsibilities of news organizations.

This perspective is relevant to immigrant-serving journalism because exposure may be intensified by community proximity. Journalists may report on immigration enforcement, political conflict, discrimination, displacement, or violence affecting communities to which they themselves belong. That proximity may generate professional knowledge and trust, but it may also collapse the boundary between occupational exposure and personal/community exposure.

The project therefore treats psychological safety and well-being as part of the wider risk ecology, while avoiding assumptions about individual mental-health outcomes that cannot be established from public evidence. Public institutional coding can identify visible support structures; the later interview phase can examine lived experience, coping, emotional labor, and organizational cultures of care.

## 6. From multidimensional safety to risk ecology

Chala's earlier article, **“From Multidimensional Safety to Risk Ecologies: A Four-Coordinate Analysis of Journalist Exposure in Ethiopia, 1992–2024”** (*Journalism Practice*, 2026), provides a direct conceptual precursor but addresses a different empirical and analytical problem.

That study combines a longitudinal event dataset with interviews to show that exposure is relational rather than additive. In the Ethiopian case, political environment, professional orientation, visibility, organizational mediation, location, state and non-state actors, and platform dynamics interact in ways that make similar threats produce different outcomes. The article therefore shifts the study of journalist safety from measuring separate dimensions toward mapping **risk ecologies**: configurations in which threats, institutional conditions, professional position, and political context interact.

The present U.S. project extends that move while changing the unit and scale of analysis.

The Ethiopia study asks primarily how **journalists' exposure changes within a national political system across time**.

The immigrant-journalism project asks how **organizationally heterogeneous news outlets occupy different risk ecologies within one national media system**.

The key shift is therefore:

> **from relational exposure among journalists to relational vulnerability among news organizations and the communities, infrastructures, and platforms through which they produce knowledge.**

This project does not simply transfer the Ethiopia framework to the United States. It uses risk ecology as a sensitizing concept and rebuilds the empirical architecture around organizational variation.

---

## 7. What this project adds

The project's contribution lies at the intersection of journalist-safety research, local-news studies, immigrant-media research, and platform/infrastructure scholarship.

Existing journalist-safety work has established that:

- safety is multidimensional;
- risks operate at micro, meso, and macro levels;
- technological infrastructures matter;
- safety measures reshape journalistic practice;
- coping and resilience are unequally distributed;
- organizations have responsibilities that cannot be shifted entirely onto individual journalists.

The present study adds a national organizational infrastructure for examining these propositions in immigrant-serving U.S. journalism.

It introduces three deliberately separate empirical layers:

1. **institutional capacity** — staffing, funding, ownership, governance, legal and safety support, network ties, platform dependence, and audience infrastructure;
2. **documented events** — publicly verifiable harms, disruptions, restrictions, and organizational responses;
3. **structural exposure** — sector-, policy-, funding-, platform-, and enforcement-level conditions that do not automatically translate into outlet-level harm.

These are not summed into a composite risk score.

Instead, the project examines the relational sequence:

> **institutional capacity → structural exposure → event or pressure → organizational response → consequence**

The model also allows feedback: responses may create new capacities, dependencies, or vulnerabilities, meaning risk ecology is dynamic rather than linear.

## 8. Epistemic precarity as the project's distinctive concept

The safety literature explains much of the project's foundation, but **epistemic precarity** is intended to identify a more specific institutional consequence.

Epistemic precarity refers to the condition in which a news organization's capacity to produce, verify, distribute, and protect community-relevant knowledge depends on unstable or uneven combinations of:

- human labor;
- financial resources;
- legal protection;
- physical and psychological safety;
- digital and platform infrastructures;
- linguistic and cultural expertise;
- source and audience trust;
- organizational support;
- access to professional and community networks.

A safety threat becomes epistemically consequential when it changes what can be known, reported, verified, translated, distributed, or safely communicated.

Examples may include:

- canceling an assignment because legal support is unavailable;
- withholding identifying information to protect undocumented sources;
- reducing publication because staff departures collapse editorial capacity;
- withdrawing from a platform because harassment becomes intolerable;
- losing referral traffic or monetization that finances reporting;
- narrowing coverage because threats create chilling effects;
- relying on unpaid or under-supported community labor to maintain information flows.

This is where the study moves beyond a conventional safety inventory. It asks how risk alters the **institutional production of knowledge for immigrant communities**.

---

## 9. Implications for the empirical design

The theoretical framework justifies the project's decision to collect institutional capacity and risk-event data in parallel but separate datasets.

A documented assault does not by itself establish organizational precarity. A small budget does not by itself establish danger. A platform account does not by itself establish platform dependence. Likewise, the absence of a publicly documented incident does not establish safety.

The analysis therefore requires relational comparison across outlets and events, followed by qualitative interviews that can identify mechanisms not visible in public records.

The public dataset provides the institutional map and observable event layer. Interviews will examine lived exposure, informal safety practices, psychological burden, chilling effects, legal uncertainty, platform pressures, community responsibility, and organizational adaptation.

## Working proposition for the manuscript

A concise theoretical proposition emerging from this literature is:

> **Journalistic risk is not reducible to the frequency or severity of threats. Its consequences are conditioned by the institutional capacities, infrastructures, dependencies, and relationships through which journalism is produced. For immigrant-serving news organizations, those conditions shape not only journalists' safety but the continuity and integrity of community knowledge production itself.**
