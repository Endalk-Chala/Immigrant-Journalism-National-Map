# Methods — Working Manuscript Draft

This study uses a multi-layered research design to examine how immigrant-serving news organizations in the United States encounter and manage institutional risk. The analysis begins with a national organizational sampling frame and then links that frame to three additional forms of evidence: internal organizational capacity, external support infrastructure, and documented risk events or structural exposures. These layers are analyzed relationally rather than collapsed into a single risk score.

The national sampling frame was built through targeted searches, community-media directories, professional associations, outlet websites, and credible secondary sources. External databases and directories were used to help identify and cross-check outlets, but they did not determine inclusion in the study. Each candidate was reviewed using the same criteria: it had to be U.S.-based or substantially serve U.S. audiences, produce sustained journalism or public-affairs content, and substantially serve immigrant, refugee, diaspora, language, ethnic, or immigrant-origin communities. This process produced 531 assessed records, including 301 core verified outlets or journalism products across all 50 states and the District of Columbia. Once the national map was completed, those 301 outlets became the study's baseline population for the next stages of data collection. Later information about staffing, funding, support networks, or risk was added as separate analytical data rather than used to change the original inclusion decisions.

A second layer documents the internal institutional characteristics of the 301 core outlets using publicly available evidence. These characteristics include organizational form, ownership and governance, newsroom staffing, revenue structure, legal and safety resources, editorial infrastructure, platform dependence, and direct-audience capacity. Public non-disclosure is not treated as evidence of absence. Where relevant information cannot be established from public sources, the study records the information as not publicly disclosed, unclear, or not yet researched rather than inferring organizational weakness from silence.

A third layer maps external support infrastructure. This layer reflects the study's working proposition that small immigrant-serving news organizations may rely on professional networks, fiscal sponsors, legal organizations, journalist-safety organizations, funders, field-building institutions, collaboratives, and other external actors for capacities that larger newsrooms may maintain internally. The unit of analysis is an outlet-to-support-organization relationship. Coding distinguishes among the existence of an affiliation, the forms of support potentially available through that relationship, and publicly documented evidence that the outlet actually used or mobilized those resources. This distinction prevents formal membership or directory presence from being treated automatically as evidence of practical protection. External support infrastructure therefore enters the analysis as a possible form of distributed organizational capacity rather than as an assumed measure of resilience.

The fourth layer documents risk events, anticipatory organizational responses, and broader structural exposures. Event records are coded separately from organizational characteristics so that the occurrence of an incident does not become a permanent label attached to an outlet. The event schema distinguishes legal, physical, political, economic, and informational domains and separately records mechanisms, actors, targets, coercion type, consequences, organizational responses, and evidence quality. Structural conditions such as platform referral decline, funding contraction, immigration-enforcement environments, or other sector-wide changes are retained as structural exposures unless outlet-specific evidence establishes a direct organizational effect.

These four layers provide the basis for purposive interview sampling. Interview cases will be selected to capture variation in organizational form, newsroom capacity, language and community orientation, geographic context, funding structure, platform dependence, internal legal and safety resources, external network embeddedness, and documented exposure or adaptation to risk. The public organizational data therefore function not only as descriptive evidence but also as the methodological foundation for selecting contrasting cases. The interview phase will then examine dimensions that public records cannot establish reliably, including whether nominal support relationships become usable resources during crises, how organizations make safety and reporting decisions, and how internal and external forms of capacity shape the consequences of risk.
